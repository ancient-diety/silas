OUR EXPERIENCE

Front Info

This page is a space for us to explain the different types of fronting we experience and use in our front logs. Fronting isn't always a straightforward experience for us. Depending on who's present, how they interact with one another, and how much control they have, our experiences can differ quite significantly.

We've established our own terminology to distinguish between these experiences. Some of these terms may be used differently by other systems, so the descriptions below refer specifically to how we experience them.

01

Fronting

Fronting refers to a split being present at the front and having control over the body and its interactions with the external world.

When one split is fronting individually, they have primary control over the body. This includes physical movements, speech, facial expressions, decision-making and general interactions with the environment.

However, fronting isn't always limited to one split at a time for us.

We can experience multiple splits fronting simultaneously, with each retaining their own ability to take control of the body. This is different from our definition of co-fronting, as control isn't necessarily shared or restricted to one particular split.

In these situations, control can move between the participating splits, sometimes very quickly. One split might be controlling the hands and legs at one moment, while another takes over those movements or controls a different part of the body the next. Their individual intentions and movements can overlap or compete with one another.

For example, three splits could be fronting together. One might be moving the legs and hands, another might suddenly want to move and take control of those actions, while the third is simultaneously attempting to use the hands.

This can become particularly complicated when multiple splits want to perform different actions at the same time. It can feel frustrating, especially when their intentions don't coordinate or when control keeps shifting between them.

Importantly, having multiple splits fronting in this way doesn't mean that they've blended together or surrendered their individual control. They remain distinguishable from one another, even when their control over the body overlaps.

In short: One split can have control over the body individually, or multiple splits can front simultaneously while retaining their own ability to control physical actions. Control can shift between them, overlap and occasionally become difficult to coordinate.

02

Co-Fronting

Co-fronting is when two or more splits are present at the front and share access to the external experience, particularly through the eyes.

Unlike our other form of simultaneous fronting, co-fronting doesn't mean that everyone involved has independent control over the body.

Generally, the split who arrived at the front first, or the split positioned foremost at the front, retains primary control over the body.

Other splits can still share the visual experience, see what's happening, follow conversations and remain present alongside the person currently controlling the body.

This means that multiple splits can experience the same external environment at once without necessarily being able to independently control physical movements or take over actions.

The distinction is that the other splits are present and sharing the fronting experience, but the person in the foremost position retains control.

In short: Multiple splits share the experience of seeing and being at the front, but the split who is foremost retains control over the body.

03

Co-Consciousness

Coming up

Co-consciousness is a form of simultaneous consciousness in which multiple splits are aware of one another and their respective experiences.

This can involve being aware of another split's thoughts, feelings, memories or actions while remaining conscious of one's own experience.

Co-consciousness does not automatically mean that multiple splits are fronting together. A split can be conscious of what's happening without having any control over the body.

In short: Multiple splits are conscious and aware of one another.

04

Co-Hosting

Co-hosting is a temporary arrangement in which one split takes on a supporting hosting role alongside the split currently fronting.

For us, co-hosting is not simply a situation where multiple splits share the title or responsibilities of being a host.

Only one split can take on the co-hosting position at a time, and this happens while another split is already at the front.

We generally experience co-hosting when the split currently fronting needs additional help managing everyday life, responsibilities or other circumstances.

This assistance might be needed for a few hours, throughout an entire day, or potentially for a longer period of time.

The co-hosting split essentially helps the fronting split manage their responsibilities and navigate situations that might otherwise become difficult to handle alone.

This doesn't necessarily mean that the co-hosting split is constantly controlling the body or replacing the person fronting. Their role is primarily supportive.

Co-hosting is therefore more about providing additional support and sharing the demands of everyday functioning than it is about sharing immediate physical control.

In short: One split takes on a temporary supporting hosting role alongside the split currently fronting, usually because that split needs help managing everyday life.

05

Blended Fronting

Blended fronting is when two or more splits overlap so closely at the front that their individual experiences, thoughts, emotions and identities become difficult to distinguish.

We sometimes describe this experience as split soup.

Rather than having clearly distinguishable splits sharing or alternating control, their individual contributions become mixed together.

This can affect how thoughts are experienced, how emotions are felt, how decisions are made and even how someone identifies themselves in the moment.

It can become difficult to determine exactly who's contributing to a particular thought or reaction, because multiple splits are overlapping so closely.

Blended fronting isn't necessarily a permanent loss of individual identity. The splits involved still have their own identities, but their experiences at the front become significantly less distinguishable.

This is different from our other forms of simultaneous fronting, where individual splits remain distinguishable and their respective positions or contributions are more apparent.

In short: Multiple splits overlap so closely that their individual thoughts, feelings and identities become difficult to separate. Our version of split soup.

06

Backseat Observing

Backseat observing is when a split is aware of what's happening at the front without actively participating in controlling the body.

We describe this as being in an observational position, almost like sitting behind the split currently fronting and watching everything unfold.

A split who is backseat observing can follow conversations, watch interactions, listen to what's being said and remain aware of the fronting split's actions.

They aren't necessarily disconnected or unaware of the external environment. In fact, they can be quite aware of everything happening.

Depending on the situation, they may also be able to communicate internally with the split at the front.

However, observing doesn't mean having direct control over the body. The fronting split remains responsible for physical actions and external interactions.

This is different from passive influence, because backseat observing involves actively being aware of and following the fronting experience.

In short: A split observes everything happening at the front without actively controlling or participating in the body's actions.

07

Passive Influence

Passive influence occurs when a split who isn't currently fronting indirectly affects the thoughts, emotions, reactions or behaviours of the split who is.

Unlike co-fronting or backseat observing, passive influence doesn't necessarily involve the influencing split being consciously present at the front.

Their influence can happen without deliberate communication or an intentional attempt to affect the fronting split.

This can manifest in several ways, including:

  • Sudden changes in mood or emotional state.
  • Thoughts or opinions that seem to come from elsewhere.
  • Unfamiliar preferences or sudden changes in interests.
  • Unexpected impulses or urges.
  • Changes in mannerisms, speech patterns or behaviour.
  • Emotional reactions that don't seem to match the fronting split's current feelings.

Passive influence can be subtle or particularly noticeable, depending on the split involved and the nature of the influence.

The split at the front might recognise that something feels different without necessarily knowing who's influencing them or why.

Importantly, being passively influenced doesn't mean that the influencing split has taken control of the body. The fronting split remains the one physically operating the body, even if their internal experience or behaviour is being affected.

In short: A split indirectly influences the thoughts, feelings or behaviours of the split currently fronting without necessarily being present or actively participating at the front.

A NOTE FROM US

These descriptions are based on our own experiences as a dissociative parts system. They represent the terminology we've developed to communicate our experiences more accurately.

Other systems may use these terms differently, experience these phenomena in different ways, or have terminology of their own. There isn't an expectation for everyone to experience fronting exactly as we do.

These terms simply help us explain what's happening internally, particularly when communicating with friends who might not otherwise understand the differences.
