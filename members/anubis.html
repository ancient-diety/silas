SOURCE FIGURE

Anubis

Anubis—known to the ancient Egyptians as Anpu (or Inpu)—is one of the oldest and most famous gods of ancient Egypt. He was the god of mummification, graves, and the afterlife. He was the ultimate protector of the dead, helping souls travel safely to the next world.

Emerging over 5,000 years ago during Egypt's earliest dynasties, he originally ruled the underworld as the supreme Lord of the Dead. Over the centuries, as the cult of Osiris grew to take over that crown, Anubis smoothly transitioned into the role of a protector and guide who prepared the deceased for eternity and ushered their souls through the underworld. Even after the fall of the Pharaohs, his popularity endured as the Greeks merged him with their own messenger god, Hermes, to create the hybrid deity Hermanubis.

In artwork, Anubis is instantly recognizable as a man with the head of a jackal or as a full canid resting on a shrine. The Egyptians chose this form because wild jackals frequently scavenged desert graves, so deifying the animal was a clever way to turn a graveyard threat into a holy protector. His signature pitch-black color did not represent death or evil, but rather the fertile, life-giving silt of the Nile River, symbolizing rebirth and physical renewal. Mythologically, Anubis became the world's first embalmer by mummifying the broken body of Osiris, establishing a ritual that human priests would copy for millennia. His most critical duty was presiding over the "Weighing of the Heart" ceremony, where he personally calibrated the scales of justice to measure a soul's goodness against the feather of truth, granting worthy spirits passage into paradise.

Domains & symbolism.

Mummification and Embalming: As the creator of the very first mummy (Osiris), Anubis was the divine patron of embalmers. Human priests wore jackal masks to channel his energy while preserving bodies. Cemetaries and Tombs: Anubis served as the ultimate guard dog of the dead. He watched over burial grounds from the desert cliffs to scare away grave robbers, wild animals, and evil spirits. MYTHOLOGY

Stories & traditions.

The Secret Parentage and AdoptionIn early Egyptian mythology: Anubis was considered the son of the sun god Ra. However, as the myths evolved, he was integrated into the famous Osiris Myth. The most widely accepted story states that Anubis was the result of a secret affair between Osiris (the king-god) and Nephthys (the goddess of darkness/mourning), who had disguised herself as her sister, Isis. Fearing the wrath of her vengeful husband Set, Nephthys abandoned the newborn infant in the desert marshes. Isis discovered the baby, adopted him as her own, and raised him to be her faithful protector and ally.

The Weighing of the Heart JudgmentIn the underworld (Duat): Anubis was the terrifying yet fair Guardian of the Scales in the Hall of Ma'at. When a deceased human soul arrived, Anubis would personally lead them to a massive golden scale. He placed the person's heart on one side and the feather of Ma'at (representing cosmic truth and justice) on the other. Anubis carefully adjusted and calibrated the scale. If the heart was heavy with sin and failed the test, the monster Ammit devoured it, erasing the soul from existence. If the heart balanced perfectly with the feather, Anubis deemed the soul worthy and presented them to Osiris to enjoy eternal paradise.

Anubis did not have a single, massive national temple like later gods, but his worship was anchored in specific regional hubs. His primary cult centre was the ancient city of Hardai, which the Greeks later renamed Cynopolis (literally "City of the Dogs"). Located in Upper Egypt, this city maintained a vast, sacred burial ground for canids, where thousands of jackals and dogs were mummified in Anubis's honour. Another major site of his worship was the Anubieion, a large temple complex and catacomb located at the royal necropolis of Saqqara, which served the ancient capital of Memphis.

In daily Egyptian life, the myth of Anubis became a physical reality inside the mummification workshops. The head priest overseeing the embalming process was known as the Heru-seshta ("Overseer of Mysteries"). During the most critical parts of the preservation process—such as the wrapping of the linens or the opening of the mouth ritual—this priest would wear a large, heavy ceramic or cartonnage mask of a jackal. By donning the mask, the priest spiritually became Anubis, ensuring that the divine protection of the god was directly applied to the corpse.

During the Ptolemaic Kingdom, when Greek rulers governed Egypt, religious ideas began to blend. The Greeks observed Anubis guiding souls through the underworld and recognized a direct parallel to their own messenger god, Hermes, who performed the same duty. The two deities were fused into Hermanubis. In Hellenistic art, this hybrid god was depicted as a man wearing Greek robes, holding the caduceus (Hermes's staff), but retaining the distinct, striking head of an Egyptian jackal.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT

Worship & representation.

Worship

  • Because Anubis was a canid god, living dogs and jackals were kept and bred in special enclosures near his temples, such as the ones found at Gebel Asyut al-gharbi. When these animals died, they were carefully mummified and wrapped in ornamental, basket-weave linen. Everyday ancient Egyptians would buy these animal mummies from the temple priests to bury them in massive catacombs as a votive offering. By paying for a dog's burial, the worshipper believed the animal would carry a personal prayer directly to Anubis, asking him to protect a deceased relative.
  • Small amulets of a crouching jackal representing Anubis were incredibly popular. While many were placed inside mummy wrappings to guard the physical body, living Egyptians also wore them or kept them in their homes as general protective charms. Because the jackal was natively a dangerous grave scavenger, owning an Anubis amulet was viewed as a spiritual insurance policy—using the image of the predator to ward off threats, sickness, and sudden death.

Representation

  • Anubis is associated with a desert canid, usually depicted as a sleek black jackal or a man with a jackal's head. The Egyptians created this link out of necessity: wild jackals naturally scavenged shallow desert graves. By turning this feared predator into a god, the Egyptians spiritually transformed a graveyard threat into a holy guardian of their dead.
  • While historically called a jackal, modern DNA testing has revealed that the physical animal that inspired Anubis's long snout and pointed ears was actually a native African wolf.
  • The Ankh and Scepter: In his human-hybrid form, he frequently holds an Ankh (the symbol of life) in one hand and a Was scepter (symbolizing power and dominion) in the other, asserting his control over the forces of the afterlife. This is not a mistake, both Seth and Anubis have this association.

SYMBOLISM

Symbols & imagery.

The Magic Funerary Brick

According to the Book of the Dead, a protective mud-brick bearing a small amulet of Anubis was placed directly into the west wall of the burial chamber. Facing east toward the mummy, it acted as a physical spiritual barrier to block evil forces from entering the tomb.

The Imiut Fetish

This sacred object consists of an stuffed, headless animal skin tied by the tail to a vertical pole. It was a powerful emblem of the embalming tent, symbolizing the preservation of the body and Anubis's mastery over physical transformation.

The Djed Pillar

Looking like a column with horizontal bars, this symbol represents stability, endurance, and the backbone of Osiris. Anubis is frequently depicted alongside it in tomb artwork to guarantee the long-term spiritual structure and protection of the deceased's soul.
