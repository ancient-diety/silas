SOURCE FIGURE

Set/Sutekh

Set (aka Seth or Sutekh) is the ancient Egyptian god of deserts, storms, disorder, violence, and foreign lands

In the earliest days of ancient Egypt, Set was seen as a force for good. People worshipped him as a friendly god of incredible raw power, masculinity, and military strength. His earliest followers lived in Upper Egypt near desert trade routes and gold mines, where they looked to Set as a protector. During this era, pharaohs loved to link themselves to Set because they wanted to borrow his ferocious strength to help them rule and win wars. He was even praised for a massive heroic act: every single night, he stood on the sun god Ra's boat and fought off a giant cosmic chaos serpent to make sure the sun could rise the next morning

However, Set's reputation took a major turn for the worse as Egypt’s history progressed. The change started to speed up when the stories about his brother Osiris became wildly popular across the country. In these myths, Set became jealous of Osiris and murdered him to steal the crown. Later on, when Egypt suffered from civil wars and invasions by foreign rulers, everyday citizens began blaming Set for all their real-world misfortunes. Because he was the god of outsiders, he was treated like a traitor. By the end of ancient Egyptian history, people had smashed Set's statues, erased his name from temple walls, and completely cast him out, viewing him as a literal demon.

Throughout his long history, Set’s major associations remained tied to the harsh, unpredictable sides of life and nature. He was officially known as the Lord of the Red Land, which meant he was the ruler of the scorching, barren deserts that surrounded the green, fertile Nile Valley. Because the desert was dangerous, Egyptians associated Set with the color red—linking it to fire, fury, and bad luck. He was also the master of sudden, terrifying natural occurrences like thunderstorms, earthquakes, and violent flash floods. Most of all, Set was associated with confusion and disorder. He was the ultimate rule-breaker who stood directly against the traditional Egyptian concept of truth, balance, and cosmic harmony.

ASSOCIATIONS

Domains & symbolism.

The Desert: Known as the Lord of the Red Land, his chief domain was the harsh, arid, and barren desert (deshret) bordering the fertile Nile valley. Storms and Chaos: He ruled over wild, unpredictable elements like thunder, lightning, fierce winds, and earthquakes, which represented cosmic disorder (isfet). Foreign Lands: Because he controlled the deserts outside of Egypt's borders, he was associated with foreign nations, outsiders, and the protection of international trade routes. War and Physical Strength: He was the patron of raw physical power, aggression, and military might, often invoked by pharaohs to crush their enemies in battle.

MYTHOLOGY

Stories & traditions.

The Murder of Osiris: Set’s most famous mythological role is as the ultimate antagonist in the Osiris myth. Consumed by jealousy over his brother’s prosperous rule, Set tricked Osiris into climbing inside a custom-made chest, sealed it shut, and threw it into the Nile. Later, when Osiris's wife, Isis, recovered the body, Set discovered it, hacked Osiris into 14 pieces, and scattered them across Egypt to prevent him from returning to life.

The Contendings of Horus and Set: Following the murder, Set fought a brutal, 80-year legal and physical battle against his nephew, Horus, for the throne of Egypt. During these intense cosmic clashes, Set tore out Horus's left eye (which became the Eye of Horus), while Horus castrated Set, stripping him of his masculine power. The divine tribunal of gods ultimately ruled in favor of Horus, casting Set out of the fertile lands.

The myths of Set’s fierce rivalry with Osiris and Horus were not just dark tragedies. They were actually a mix of deep cosmic politics and surprisingly comedic, chaotic courtroom drama.A fascinating detail is that ancient Egyptian texts and art never directly describe or show Set murdering Osiris. Because Egyptians believed that writing down an event could magically cause it to happen again, explicitly detailing the death of a king was a massive taboo. Instead, they used vague hints, focusing entirely on Osiris's rebirth. When it came to the fight for the throne, the "Contendings" were recorded as an 80-year satirical legal battle before the council of gods. The divine judges were completely deadlocked. The sun god Ra strongly favored Set for his age and raw muscle, while the other gods favored Horus as the rightful heir. The court became so petty that Ra actually threw a temper tantrum and pouted alone in his tent when the council refused to vote for Set.To break the legal stalemate, Set challenged Horus to absurd, rigged contests, but he was constantly outsmarted. In one challenge, Set demanded a race using boats carved from heavy stone. Horus secretly built his boat out of wood and painted it to look like stone, leaving Set's real stone vessel to sink straight to the bottom of the Nile.The ultimate turning point involved a bizarre psychological trick. Set tried to assert dominance over Horus, but Isis intercepted him. She secretly spread Horus's essence over Set's favorite patch of romaine lettuce. Set unknowingly ate the lettuce, and when the gods later held a magical trial, Horus's seed answered from inside Set, bursting out of his forehead as a golden solar disc. This public humiliation effectively ended Set's claims to the throne, sealing Horus's victory.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT

Worship & representation.

Worship

  • Set’s earliest and most enduring sanctuary was Nubt (known as Ombos in Greek), located near the modern town of Naqada in Upper Egypt. Because Nubt was a wealthy predynastic center controlling gold mines and desert trade routes, Set was worshiped there as "He of Nubt," the supreme lord of the southern lands.Centuries later, during the Second Intermediate Period and New Kingdom, his primary center shifted north to Avaris (and later the grand royal city of Pi-Ramesses) in the Nile Delta. Here, Set was embraced as a warrior god who protected the frontier borders. His worship also thrived in the provincial capital of Sepermeru in Middle Egypt, where he shared a prominent sanctuary called the "House of Set, Lord of Sepermeru" next to temples of other primary gods
  • In daily religious practice, Set was viewed with profound ambivalence, representing a "necessary evil" or a force of constructive disruption. In the early eras and the New Kingdom, temple rituals focused on harnessing his aggressive, untamed power to bless the pharaoh with supreme victory over foreign enemies. However, as his mythology darkened over the centuries, his mainstream ritual context shifted dramatically toward execration and pacification. Instead of praying to him for favor, priests performed highly structured rituals designed to bind, curse, or neutralize his volatile anger so it wouldn't disrupt maat (cosmic order). This gave rise to sacrificial temple rituals where animals linked to Set—such as red oxen, wild boes, donkeys, or hippopotamuses—were bound or slaughtered to symbolically crush the chaos he commanded.

Representation

  • The Set Animal (known as the sha) is a mysterious, unidentified beast that served as the physical form and hieroglyphic symbol of the god Set (𓁣)
  • The iconography of Set was specifically crafted to convey danger, power, and a defiance of nature through unique shapes, aggressive colors, and chaotic animals. His most common representation is a hybrid form showing a man with the head of the mythical Set Animal. This unnatural head features an elongated, downward-curving muzzle and tall, rigidly upright, square-tipped ears that set him apart from any real animal. When drawn entirely as a beast, he takes the form of a slender canine with a stiff, straight tail that stands up at an angle and ends in a distinct fork. Color symbolism was incredibly important to his identity, and he was heavily tied to the color red, which the Egyptians associated with the blazing desert sun, rage, and bad luck. Artists frequently painted him with bright red skin, red hair, or piercing red eyes to immediately signal danger.In his hands, he typically holds the ankh symbol of life and the Was-scepter of power. Interestingly, the top of this royal staff is visually shaped exactly like the head of the Set Animal itself.

SYMBOLISM

Symbols & imagery.

Iron (Meteoric Iron)

In ancient Egyptian thought, iron was structurally known as "the bones of Set." Because iron was a hard, cold metal that fell unpredictably from the sky via meteorites, they spiritually linked it to the god of cosmic storms and unmatched physical strength.

The Black Pig or Boar

This animal symbolized Set’s underhanded tactics and spiritual uncleanness. In mythology, Set transformed himself into a ferocious black boar to attack the sun god Ra's eye and later used the same form to hunt down the hidden body of Osiris.

The Male Hippopotamus

While the female hippo was seen as a peaceful symbol of fertility, the aggressive male hippopotamus was a major symbol for Set. It perfectly captured his role as a dangerous, destructive force capable of capsizing boats and causing chaos along the Nile.
